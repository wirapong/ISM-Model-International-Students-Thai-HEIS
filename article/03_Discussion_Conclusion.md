## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 Overview of Key Findings

This study examined how two institutional quality constructs — Academic and Education Quality (AR) and Administrative and Staff Support (HS) — influence four student outcome constructs — Psychological Adaptation (PA), Sociocultural Adaptation (LC), Information Needs satisfaction (IA), and Situational Issue management (SI) — among 354 international students in Thai higher education institutions. Using PLS-SEM with 5,000 bootstrap resamples, seven of the eight hypothesized structural paths were supported (p < 0.05), and the model explained substantial variance in Sociocultural Adaptation (R² = 0.838) and Situational Issue management (R² = 0.862), and moderate variance in Psychological Adaptation (R² = 0.578) and Information Needs (R² = 0.557). These results collectively affirm the theoretical proposition that institutional quality dimensions operate as direct antecedents across the full spectrum of international students' adaptation and informational experiences.

### 5.2 Interpretation of Supported Paths

#### 5.2.1 Academic and Education Quality (AR) → All Four Outcomes (H1a–H1d)

Academic and Education Quality exerted statistically significant positive effects on all four endogenous constructs, supporting H1a–H1d. The largest effect was on Sociocultural Adaptation (β = 0.483, f² = 0.573, large), followed by Information Needs satisfaction (β = 0.469, f² = 0.174, medium), Psychological Adaptation (β = 0.416, f² = 0.164, medium), and Situational Issue management (β = 0.308, f² = 0.273, medium–large).

These findings are consistent with and extend prior evidence. The finding that academic quality positively predicts sociocultural adaptation aligns with Chang et al.'s (2020) observation that students in higher-quality academic environments report greater competence in navigating local social norms, as rich academic interactions provide socialization opportunities that facilitate cultural integration. The effect of academic quality on psychological adaptation corroborates Martirosyan's (2019) finding that academic engagement and achievement are key buffers against psychological distress among international students; when students perceive their academic environment as intellectually stimulating and well-resourced, they report greater psychological well-being (Bethel et al., 2020). The significant effect on information needs satisfaction extends prior work by Hertzum and Hyldegård (2019) by demonstrating that academic quality — including library access, digital resources, and instructional support — is a structural antecedent to how effectively students' information requirements are met. The effect on situational issue management, while smaller in magnitude (β = 0.308), is theoretically important: it suggests that strong academic infrastructure reduces the practical obstacles international students encounter in their daily academic lives, consistent with Kang et al.'s (2019) observation that curriculum clarity and academic support reduce navigational difficulties for Chinese students in Thailand.

#### 5.2.2 Administrative and Staff Support (HS) → Outcomes (H3a, H3b, H3d)

Administrative and Staff Support exerted the strongest effect in the entire model on Situational Issue management (β = 0.669, f² = 1.291, very large), a large effect on Sociocultural Adaptation (β = 0.490, f² = 0.598), and a medium effect on Psychological Adaptation (β = 0.392, f² = 0.146). These findings support H3a, H3b, and H3d.

The very large effect of administrative support on Situational Issue management (f² = 1.291) is the most substantively notable finding of this study. It indicates that responsive, competent administrative services are the single most powerful institutional lever for reducing the practical and contextual challenges faced by international students in Thailand. This aligns with and amplifies the findings of Veerasamy and Ammigan (2022), who identified staff training, remote service access, and proactive communication as critical institutional responses to international student challenges, and with Madden-Dent et al.'s (2019) finding that the cultural knowledge and health and safety components of student support services are essential for navigating situational difficulties. The magnitude of this effect (f² > 1.0, far exceeding the "large" threshold of 0.35) suggests that in the Thai context, where institutional culture may be less familiar to international students than in Anglophone destinations, administrative responsiveness operates as a particularly powerful situational safety net.

The large effect of administrative support on sociocultural adaptation (β = 0.490, f² = 0.598) is consistent with Jampaklay et al.'s (2022) argument that institutions must actively promote cross-cultural programming through administrative channels to facilitate integration, and with Sangkhao et al.'s (2019) finding that sociocultural adaptation in Thailand is closely tied to the quality of institutional social support structures. The medium effect on psychological adaptation (β = 0.392, f² = 0.146) aligns with Moscaritolo et al.'s (2022) finding that institutional support services are critical for international students' mental well-being, particularly in contexts of uncertainty.

#### 5.2.3 Comparison with Prior ISM Research

Taken together, the pattern of supported paths reveals an important asymmetry in the institutional factors' predictive profiles. Academic and Education Quality exerts its strongest effects on adaptation and informational outcomes through perceived content quality and resource richness, operating primarily through cognitive and informational channels. Administrative and Staff Support, by contrast, exerts its dominant effect on situational issue management through interpersonal responsiveness and practical problem-solving support, operating primarily through social and procedural channels. This distinction extends the SERVQUAL framework (Parasuraman et al., 1988) to the Thai ISM context by demonstrating that academic quality (analogous to SERVQUAL's "reliability" and "tangibles" dimensions) and administrative support (analogous to "responsiveness" and "empathy") do not merely duplicate each other's effects but activate different pathways to student success. This differentiated profile has not been previously documented in the Thai international higher education literature and constitutes a theoretical contribution of this study.

### 5.3 Interpretation of the Non-Significant Path: HS → IA (H3c)

The only non-supported hypothesis was H3c: Administrative and Staff Support did not significantly predict Information Needs satisfaction (β = 0.322, p = 0.104, f² = 0.113). This finding warrants careful interpretation.

One plausible explanation is **statistical suppression**: when Academic and Education Quality (AR) is simultaneously included as a predictor of IA, its stronger and statistically robust effect (β = 0.469, p = 0.016) may absorb the variance in IA that would otherwise be attributable to HS. This interpretation is consistent with the high bivariate correlation implied by HTMT = 0.690 between HS and IA: the zero-order relationship between administrative support and information needs may be meaningful, but it is largely explained by the shared variance with academic quality in the multivariate model. A hierarchical or sequential approach in future research could clarify this pattern.

A second, theoretically important interpretation is that international students in Thailand primarily associate information needs satisfaction with the academic environment — the library, faculty, curriculum, and digital resources — rather than with administrative offices. Administrative staff may be perceived as addressing procedural matters (registration, visas, housing) rather than intellectual or academic information requirements. This aligns with Yoon and Chung's (2017) finding that international students differentiate clearly between academic information sources and administrative information channels in their information-seeking strategies. The implication is that universities wishing to improve information needs satisfaction should prioritize academic quality investments over administrative communications strategies.

### 5.4 R² and Predictive Relevance

The model's explanatory power is particularly high for Sociocultural Adaptation (R² = 0.838) and Situational Issue management (R² = 0.862), indicating that together, Academic and Education Quality and Administrative and Staff Support account for over 83% of the variance in these two outcome domains. This is notably higher than reported in similar single-dimension studies (e.g., Arambewela & Hall, 2009, who reported R² values of 0.35–0.50 in student satisfaction models limited to one institutional factor). The moderate R² values for Psychological Adaptation (0.578) and Information Needs (0.557) indicate that institutional factors account for approximately half the variance in these outcomes, suggesting that individual-level factors — such as prior intercultural experience, language proficiency, and personality characteristics — also contribute substantially and represent productive targets for future inquiry.

### 5.5 Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings.

**First**, the AVE of the Situational Issue (SI) construct was 0.485, marginally below the 0.50 convergent validity threshold. While all three SI items were retained on content validity grounds and their bootstrapped loadings were statistically significant (all *p* < 0.001), this limitation suggests that SI may not fully capture the domain-specific variance implied by the construct definition. Future research should refine the SI measurement instrument to include additional items that better represent the breadth of situational challenges faced by international students in Thailand, with the aim of achieving AVE ≥ 0.50.

**Second**, the HTMT ratio between Administrative and Staff Support (HS) and Situational Issue (SI) was 0.904, marginally exceeding the 0.90 discriminant validity threshold. This suggests partial conceptual overlap between these two constructs, which is interpretively meaningful — administrative support structures directly reduce situational challenges — but also signals that the two constructs are not entirely empirically distinct. This overlap may partly explain the very large effect size of HS on SI (f² = 1.291). Future research could address this by developing more theoretically differentiated measurement instruments that separate the administrative *input* domain from the situational *outcome* domain.

**Third**, the exclusion of the Image and Prestige (HT) construct, necessitated by its near-perfect discriminant validity failure with Administrative and Staff Support (HTMT = 0.955), limits the study's coverage of the full Student Satisfaction dimension. This finding itself carries a theoretical implication: in the Thai international higher education context, students' perceptions of institutional image and administrative support may be experientially inseparable, suggesting that institutional prestige is perceived primarily through administrative service encounters rather than through symbolic or reputational signals. Future research using qualitative methods could illuminate the mechanisms underlying this overlap.

**Fourth**, data were collected from a single country (Thailand) using cross-sectional self-report measures, limiting the generalizability of findings to other ASEAN or Asian higher education contexts and precluding causal inference in the strict experimental sense. Longitudinal designs tracking students' adaptation over time would provide stronger evidence for the causal ordering of the ISM model's constructs.

**Fifth**, the Direct/Parallel structural model specification, while theoretically justified for exploring simultaneous institutional effects, does not capture potential mediation pathways — for example, whether sociocultural adaptation mediates the relationship between academic quality and information needs. Future research employing sequential mediation models could reveal the full causal chain linking institutional quality inputs to student outcomes.

## 6. Conclusion

### 6.1 Summary of Findings

This study developed and empirically tested an Information Services Management (ISM) model for international students in Thai higher education, integrating three theoretical dimensions — Student Satisfaction, Cultural Adjustment, and Student Needs — into a unified PLS-SEM framework. Using data from 354 international students across four types of Thai universities, the analysis demonstrated that two institutional quality factors, Academic and Education Quality (AR) and Administrative and Staff Support (HS), are robust and broad-reaching antecedents of international students' experiences. Seven of eight hypothesized structural paths were statistically supported, with Administrative and Staff Support emerging as the dominant predictor of Situational Issue management (β = 0.669, f² = 1.291) and Academic and Education Quality as the strongest predictor of Sociocultural Adaptation (β = 0.483, f² = 0.573). The model explained substantial variance in Sociocultural Adaptation (R² = 0.838) and Situational Issue management (R² = 0.862), confirming the strong predictive utility of institutional quality factors. The only non-supported path — Administrative and Staff Support to Information Needs satisfaction (β = 0.322, p = 0.104) — suggests that students associate information needs fulfillment primarily with the academic, rather than administrative, dimensions of their institutional experience.

### 6.2 Theoretical Contributions

This study makes three theoretical contributions to the ISM and international higher education literatures. First, it demonstrates that institutional quality factors operate as simultaneous, direct predictors across the full spectrum of international students' adaptation and informational outcomes, integrating Student Satisfaction, Cultural Adjustment, and Student Needs dimensions that prior research has treated separately. Second, it reveals a theoretically meaningful asymmetry in the predictive profiles of academic quality and administrative support, extending the SERVQUAL framework by showing that these two institutional dimensions activate different pathways — cognitive/informational versus social/procedural — to student success. Third, the near-failure of discriminant validity between institutional image and administrative support (HTMT = 0.955) suggests that in the Thai context, students construct institutional prestige perceptions primarily through their administrative service encounters, a finding that challenges the conventional treatment of image and service quality as distinct constructs in international student research.

### 6.3 Practical Implications

The findings carry actionable implications for Thai higher education institutions and national policy.

**For university administrators**, the most impactful investment for improving international students' overall experience is **responsive, competent administrative and staff support** — particularly in reducing the situational barriers students face (HS → SI: β = 0.669, f² = 1.291). This means prioritizing: staff training in intercultural communication and international student needs; streamlining administrative procedures for registration, visa support, and housing; creating dedicated international student service centers staffed by personnel with multilingual capability; and ensuring rapid response to student inquiries and complaints. These administrative investments offer the highest return in terms of reducing situational challenges and promoting cultural integration simultaneously.

**For academic program developers and faculty**, the finding that academic quality significantly predicts all four outcome domains — including sociocultural adaptation and information needs satisfaction — underscores the importance of curriculum design that actively incorporates cross-cultural content, diverse peer collaboration opportunities, and robust information literacy support. Universities should ensure that academic programs include English-medium instruction with adequate language support, international-standard library and digital resources, and structured academic mentoring for international students.

**For the Office of Higher Education Commission (OHEC) and national policy**, the ISM model provides an evidence-based framework for evaluating and benchmarking institutional readiness to serve international students. OHEC could develop standardized quality indicators aligned with the six constructs of the ISM model — particularly the two institutional input factors (AR and HS) — as a basis for accreditation criteria, institutional rankings, and performance-based funding for international student services in Thai universities. The finding that image and administrative support are empirically indistinguishable from students' perspective further suggests that institutional reputation building should be pursued through service excellence rather than symbolic marketing alone.

### 6.4 Directions for Future Research

Building on this study's findings and limitations, the following directions are proposed for future research.

**First**, future studies should test a **sequential mediation model** in which Psychological Adaptation and Sociocultural Adaptation mediate the relationships between institutional quality inputs (AR and HS) and information outcomes (IA and SI). Such a model would reveal whether cultural and psychological adaptation is a necessary precondition for satisfying international students' information needs and managing situational challenges, or whether institutional quality inputs exert their effects through direct pathways only.

**Second**, the SI and IA measurement instruments should be refined through qualitative item development — including focus groups and cognitive interviews with international students in Thailand — to improve content coverage and achieve adequate AVE, particularly for SI. Future versions of the ISM model might include sub-dimensions of situational issues (e.g., financial, language, social) and differentiated types of information needs (academic, administrative, social, legal).

**Third**, this study focused exclusively on Thai higher education. Cross-national comparative studies examining ISM models in other ASEAN destinations — such as Malaysia, Vietnam, or Singapore — would reveal whether the structural relationships identified here are context-specific or generalizable across the region, contributing to a broader ASEAN international education theory.

**Fourth**, longitudinal designs tracking the same cohort of international students at multiple time points (e.g., upon arrival, mid-semester, end of program) would provide stronger evidence for the temporal precedence of institutional quality factors and allow examination of how adaptation trajectories evolve over the course of a student's studies in Thailand.

**Fifth**, future research should explicitly examine the role of **student nationality and language background** as moderators of the ISM model's structural relationships. Given that the majority of international students in Thailand are Chinese nationals, it remains unclear whether the model's pathways generalize to students from other linguistic and cultural backgrounds (e.g., CLMV countries, South Asian students, or Western exchange students).
